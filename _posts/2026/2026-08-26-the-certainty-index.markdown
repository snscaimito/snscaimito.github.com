## The Steady Number

Elena was twenty-two, and every year of her life through seventy-eight had already been settled.

Every morning at six forty-five, the apartment chime sounded once—a rounded, polite tone, as if even waking should involve no shock. Elena reached for her phone on the standard-issue nightstand. The screen showed no breaking news, no urgent messages. Only the national harmony glyph and her personal Certainty Index: **41.8%**.

The number was not supposed to be a grade. It was a promise, the kind adults lowered their voices around. School, the civic aptitude review, two years in the Municipal Cadastre pool: each obedient step had turned a little more of her future green. If she accepted the intake desk assignment next month, the index would jump to sixty percent overnight. By thirty, seventy-five. By fifty, ninety-nine. A whole life converted into decimals before she had chosen how to spend a single morning.

Around her, four million young people built their lives around that number. They compared decimal points on commuter trams with quiet, satisfied smiles. Nobody went hungry. Nobody slept on the pavement. The apartments were warm in November and cool in July, even if the hot water took three minutes to run clear and the radiators hummed with a low metallic vibration that never quite stopped.

Elena walked to the window. Across the courtyard, hundreds of identical kitchen windows glowed with identical pale yellow light. People were boiling the standard blend of chicory tea and spreading subsidized butter onto gray bread.

There was no tyrant on the wall, no secret police vans idling on the corners. There was only the great, frictionless consensus: life was a dangerous machine, and the state had kindly padded all the corners.

She looked down at her hands on the cold windowsill. Her pulse beat fast beneath the skin, the only thing in the courtyard that refused to keep time with the building.

## The Banquet of Pity

By Sunday, Elena had almost convinced herself that the restless pulse meant nothing. Then her family gathered in her aunt’s flat to celebrate her cousin Jonas. He had done what every good son was raised to do: received his lifetime appointment as a junior registrar in the Department of Weights and Measures.

The table was covered with a clean vinyl cloth. There were bowls of salted cabbage, boiled potatoes, and two bottles of regional cider that tasted faintly of sweet vinegar.

"Forty-two years of guaranteed tenancy in Sector Four," Jonas’s father said, tapping his fork against his glass. His eyes were moist with genuine gratitude. "A level-two health allowance. In twenty years, an upgrade to a corner balcony."

"You did well, Jonas," her mother said softly. "Real peace. No surprises."

Jonas beamed. Relief loosened his shoulders until he seemed younger, almost boyish. It was so naked and sincere that Elena felt ashamed for resenting it.

Then her aunt turned to Elena. The room softened. The smiles did not disappear; they became tender, worried, and unbearable.

"Elena, darling," her aunt murmured. "The registry deadline is Friday. You haven’t confirmed your preference form."

"I was thinking about waiting," Elena said. She kept her voice even, but the silence that followed was as heavy as wet wool. "A year. Maybe taking temporary work in the rail yards."

Her mother did not raise her voice. That would have been easier. Instead, she reached across the table and placed a warm, dry hand over Elena’s knuckles.

"Why make things rough on yourself, Elena?" her mother asked, her voice trembling with pure, unselfish pity. "You have good marks. You can have an indoor desk. Why do you want friction?"

Elena looked around the table. Every face held love and utter incomprehension. They were not trying to cage her. They were trying to save her from a cliff only she could not see.

That was the worst of it. She loved them back.

## The 3:17 AM Anomaly

The silence from dinner followed Elena home and climbed into bed beside her.

The algorithm did not hate beauty. It simply preferred the kind that asked nothing of anyone.

She lay awake in the dark, scrolling through the stream. The national feed carried her along on pastel tones: twenty-second clips of municipal gardens, certified recipes for lentil stew, gentle breathing exercises narrated by soothing synthetic voices, and instructional animations on how to properly fold state-issued tax receipts.

It was designed to lower the heart rate. It was designed to make sleep inevitable.

Then, at three seventeen in the morning, the feed slipped.

A video appeared without a municipal watermark. The color struck her first: hard white light, black grease, brown leather. A handheld camera shook beneath a buzzing fluorescent tube in what looked like an uninsulated corrugated tin shed.

A young man with grease smudged across his cheek stood over a modified sewing machine. He was not talking about stability. His voice was fast, ragged, alive.

"They tell you the thread breaks because the tension is set by code," he said, holding up a heavy leather boot with uneven, double-reinforced brass stitching. "The thread breaks because nobody has touched a needle with their own fingers in thirty years. I made twelve pairs of these this week. Sold them behind the coal depot for six silver tokens each. You want boots that last ten winters? You build them yourself."

Nineteen seconds. That was all.

Elena stared at the screen, her heart hammering as if she had been caught doing something. The man’s eyes were bloodshot with exhaustion, but there was an electric, dangerous clarity in them that she had never seen in any registrar’s office. He had made something real. Someone had wanted it. They had faced each other and agreed on what it was worth.

The view count in the bottom corner was spinning like an electric meter: eight thousand, twenty-two thousand, fifty-one thousand.

For six minutes, Elena was not alone.

## The Clean Sweep

Elena tapped the profile icon before she could frighten herself out of it. The handle was a random string of numbers: *user_992014_raw*. Three other clips waited there: one explaining how to weld bicycle frames from scrapped municipal scaffolding, another showing how to culture sourdough without state-approved starter packs, and a third calculating profit margins on hand-ground mustard seeds.

None of it was forbidden. Not in words. A person could sew a boot or grind mustard. But everyone understood the distance between *legal* and *safe*. These people were uncoordinated. They set their own prices. They made promises no office had approved and trusted strangers to keep them.

Elena watched the comment section scroll faster than she could read:

*—Where is the workshop?*  
*—Is this allowed?*  
*—How did you calculate the token value?*  
*—My grandfather had boots like that.*  
*—Delete this before it hurts your index.*

She pressed the save button. The app answered with the same gentle chime that woke her every morning.

Then she refreshed the page.

A small circular spinner made two perfect turns against the black background. Then the man vanished. The profile link returned a pale blue notice: *“This content has been retired to maintain the Harmony Baseline.”*

In its place, the algorithm served a three-minute video of a polite woman in a beige cardigan explaining how to arrange potted ferns on an apartment balcony to maximize afternoon shade.

No sirens sounded in the street. No one kicked in a door. The system did not need anger; it simply smoothed the water until no ripples remained. The fire had burned for six minutes, and now the room was comfortably gray again.

Elena sat in the dark with her thumb pressed against the place where the save icon had been.

## The Secret Balance

Elena did not sleep. At four, she crossed the room and opened the wardrobe.

Behind her standard gray winter coats sat a shallow cedar cigar box she had found three years ago in an abandoned rail shed. Inside lay a pair of brass jeweler’s pliers, four tiny screwdrivers, and six mechanical wristwatches with shattered crystals and rusted balance wheels.

The state kept perfect time. Every clock bowed to the central radio tower once every twelve minutes so that no office differed from another by even a quarter of a second. Mechanical watches were useless artifacts—noisy, inaccurate, and prone to human error.

Their uselessness was what she loved.

Elena picked up the smallest screwdriver.

For eight months, she had been rebuilding them. She pulled tiny balance springs from old kitchen timers and ground replacement brass teeth with a diamond nail file. When she finished a movement, she traded it to an old man who lived in the basement beside the boiler room. He was a retired tram driver, and for every watch that ticked, he gave her two pounds of real dried apples and a jar of homemade walnut oil.

It was not about the apples. It was about the weight of the trade.

When the old man handed her the jar, there was no form to stamp, no supervisor to notify, and no pension index to update. He held the watch to his ear and smiled. Elena carried the oil upstairs. Nothing had been guaranteed, and both of them had kept their word.

Holding the cold brass gear between her fingertips, Elena felt her pulse quicken. It was as if the tiny machine had found the rhythm in her that the city could not quiet.

Before she closed the box, she set aside one working watch.

## The Unpaved Step

By Friday morning, Elena wore the watch beneath her coat sleeve. Its faint ticking followed her across a city washed flat beneath a silver sky.

The grand plaza in front of the Intake Pavilion was filled with hundreds of young graduates. They stood in neat, orderly rows between polished chrome guideposts, holding their blue allocation vouchers against their chests. The air smelled of clean rain, damp wool, and institutional floor wax.

Every few seconds, a bell chimed above the brass doors, and the queue stepped forward six inches in perfect unison.

Elena stood at position number forty-seven. Against her wrist, the watch gained almost four seconds every hour. In her pocket, her phone vibrated with a gentle, encouraging reminder: *“Your Certainty Index will reach 60.0% upon check-in.”*

The boy ahead of her was nineteen, with freshly cut hair and polished shoes. He wore the peaceful expression of someone who had just been told he need not fear the next fifty years. Elena understood that relief. That was why it hurt.

Elena looked past him, through the tall glass doors of the pavilion. Inside, rows of clerks sat behind double-pane glass, stamping vouchers with rhythmic, muffled thuds. The light inside was warm, yellow, and completely still.

Elena felt the blue voucher between her fingers. Its edges had gone soft beneath her thumb. At breakfast, her mother had pressed it into her hand, kissed her forehead, and held on one second too long.

The bell chimed again. The boy ahead of her took a step forward.

Elena did not.

She stepped to the left, unhooked the velvet guide rope, and ducked beneath it.

A supervisor with a clipboard looked up, his brow furrowing with mild, benevolent concern. "Miss? The entrance is straight ahead. You’re losing your position in the sequence."

"I know," Elena said.

She did not run. Running would look like panic, and she was not panicked. She turned her back on the brass doors, walked across the wet stone plaza, and headed down the narrow, unpaved alleyway where the old delivery vans were parked and the smell of hot metal and diesel hung heavy in the damp air.

Her phone chimed in her pocket as the Certainty Index fell toward zero, into a future the screen could no longer describe.

Behind her, the phone kept chiming. Against her wrist, the watch kept time.

One sound counted what she was losing. The other counted nothing but the seconds that were hers.
