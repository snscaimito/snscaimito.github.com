## 1. The Steady Number

Elena was twenty-two years old, and her life had already been settled to the age of seventy-eight.

Every morning at six forty-five, the apartment chime sounded once—a rounded, polite tone designed not to startle the heart. Elena reached for her phone from the standard-issue nightstand. The screen did not show breaking news or urgent messages. It displayed the national harmony glyph and her personal Certainty Index: **41.8%**.

The number was not a grade; it was a promise. By completing her primary schooling, passing the basic civic aptitude review, and entering the two-year preparatory pool for the Municipal Cadastre, she had secured nearly forty-two percent of her lifetime retirement guarantee. If she accepted the intake desk assignment next month, the index would jump to sixty percent overnight. By thirty, seventy-five. By fifty, ninety-nine.

Around her, four million young people lived for that number. They compared decimal points on commuter trams with quiet, satisfied smiles. Nobody went hungry. Nobody slept on the pavement. The apartments had warm radiators in November and cool air in July, even if the hot water took three minutes to run clear and the radiators hummed with a low, metallic vibration that never quite stopped.

Elena walked to the window. Across the courtyard, hundreds of identical kitchen windows glowed with identical pale yellow light. People were boiling the standard blend of chicory tea and spreading subsidized butter onto gray bread.

There was no tyrant on the wall, no secret police vans idling on the corners. There was only the great, frictionless consensus: life was a dangerous machine, and the state had kindly padded all the corners.

She looked down at her hands on the cold windowsill. Her pulse was fast, sharp, and entirely uncoordinated with the building.

## 2. The Banquet of Pity

On Sunday afternoon, Elena’s family gathered in her aunt’s flat to celebrate her cousin Jonas. Jonas had done what every good son was raised to do: he had received his lifetime appointment as a junior registrar in the Department of Weights and Measures.

The table was covered with a clean vinyl cloth. There were bowls of salted cabbage, boiled potatoes, and two bottles of regional cider that tasted faintly of sweet vinegar.

"Forty-two years of guaranteed tenancy in Sector Four," Jonas’s father said, tapping his fork against his glass. His eyes were moist with genuine gratitude. "A level-two health allowance. In twenty years, an upgrade to a corner balcony."

"You did well, Jonas," her mother said softly. "Real peace. No surprises."

Jonas beamed, his shoulders sinking into the comfortable slope of a man who would never have to make another unscripted choice for the rest of his days.

Then her aunt turned to Elena. The room softened. The smiles did not disappear; they became tender, worried, and unbearable.

"Elena, darling," her aunt murmured. "The registry deadline is Friday. You haven’t confirmed your preference form."

"I was thinking about waiting," Elena said. She kept her voice even, but the silence that followed was as heavy as wet wool. "A year. Maybe taking temporary work in the rail yards."

Her mother didn’t yell. In their world, nobody yelled. Anger was considered a crude breakdown of social coordination. Instead, her mother reached across the table and placed a warm, dry hand over Elena’s knuckles.

"Why make things rough on yourself, Elena?" her mother asked, her voice trembling with pure, unselfish pity. "You have good marks. You can have an indoor desk. Why do you want friction?"

Elena looked around the table. Every face was filled with love and utter incomprehension. To them, wanting to step into the open wind was not rebellion; it was a tragic, embarrassing mental illness.

## 3. The 3:17 AM Anomaly

The algorithm did not hate beauty; it simply preferred sedation.

Elena lay awake in the dark, scrolling through the stream. The national feed was an endless river of pastel tones: twenty-second clips of municipal gardens, certified recipes for lentil stew, gentle breathing exercises narrated by soothing synthetic voices, and instructional animations on how to properly fold state-issue tax receipts.

It was designed to lower the heart rate. It was designed to make sleep inevitable.

Then, at three seventeen in the morning, the feed slipped.

A video appeared without a municipal watermark. It had no soothing pastel filter. It was shot on a handheld camera under the harsh, buzzing glow of a fluorescent tube in what looked like an uninsulated corrugated tin shed.

A young man with grease smudged across his cheek was standing over a modified sewing machine. He was not talking about stability. His voice was fast, ragged, and loud.

"They tell you the thread breaks because the tension is set by code," he said, holding up a heavy leather boot with uneven, double-reinforced brass stitching. "The thread breaks because nobody has touched a needle with their own fingers in thirty years. I made twelve pairs of these this week. Sold them behind the coal depot for six silver tokens each. You want boots that last ten winters? You build them yourself."

The clip lasted exactly nineteen seconds.

Elena stared at the screen. The man’s eyes were bloodshot with exhaustion, but there was an electric, dangerous clarity in them that she had never seen in any registrar’s office. He was not waiting for an allocation. He had made something real, found someone who needed it, and looked them in the eye across a deal.

The view count in the bottom corner was spinning like an electric meter: eight thousand, twenty-two thousand, fifty-one thousand.

## 4. The Clean Sweep

Elena tapped the profile icon. The handle was a random string of numbers: *user_992014_raw*. There were three other clips on the channel: one explaining how to weld bicycle frames from scrapped municipal scaffolding, another showing how to culture sourdough without state-approved starter packs, and a third calculating profit margins on hand-ground mustard seeds.

None of it was illegal by formal statute. The state had no law explicitly forbidding a person from sewing a boot or grinding mustard. But it was *uncoordinated*. It introduced variance, pricing discrepancies, and unmonitored human energy into a system built entirely on predictable equilibrium.

Elena watched the comment section scroll faster than she could read:

*—Where is the workshop?*  
*—Is this allowed?*  
*—How did you calculate the token value?*  
*—My grandfather had boots like that.*  
*—Delete this before it hurts your index.*

She pressed the save button. The app gave a gentle chime.

Then she refreshed the page.

A small circular spinner turned twice against the black background. When the feed reloaded, the video was gone. The profile link returned a pale blue notice: *“This content has been retired to maintain the Harmony Baseline.”*

In its place, the algorithm served a three-minute video of a polite woman in a beige cardigan explaining how to arrange potted ferns on an apartment balcony to maximize afternoon shade.

There were no sirens in the street. No armored trucks drove past. The system did not need to crush dissent; it simply smoothed the water until no ripples remained. The fire had burned for six minutes, and now the room was comfortably gray again.

## 5. The Secret Balance

Elena did not sleep. She sat up, swung her legs over the bed, and walked quietly to the wardrobe.

Behind her standard gray winter coats, nestled in the dark recess behind the shoe box, was a shallow cedar cigar box she had found three years ago in an abandoned rail shed. Inside lay a pair of brass jeweler’s pliers, four tiny screwdrivers, and six mechanical wristwatches with shattered crystals and rusted balance wheels.

In their world, all time was digital, synchronized every twelve minutes by the central radio tower so that no office clock differed from another by even a quarter of a second. Mechanical watches were considered useless artifacts—noisy, inaccurate, and prone to human error.

Elena picked up the smallest screwdriver.

For the past eight months, she had been rebuilding them. She sourced tiny balance springs from old kitchen timers and ground replacement brass teeth using a diamond nail file. When she finished a movement, she did not keep it on her wrist. She traded it to an old man who lived in the basement beside the boiler room—a retired tram driver who gave her two pounds of real dried apples and a jar of homemade walnut oil for every watch that ticked.

It was not about the apples. It was about the weight of the trade.

When the old man handed her the jar, there was no form to stamp, no supervisor to notify, and no pension index to update. It was a transaction born of pure human agreement: *I made this, you value it, we agree.*

Holding the cold brass gear between her fingertips, Elena felt her pulse again. It was the only part of her week that did not feel like anesthesia.

## 6. The Unpaved Step

Friday morning arrived under a flat, silver sky.

The grand plaza in front of the Intake Pavilion was filled with hundreds of young graduates. They stood in neat, orderly rows between polished chrome guideposts, holding their blue allocation vouchers against their chests. The air smelled of clean rain, damp wool, and institutional floor wax.

Every few seconds, a bell chimed above the brass doors, and the queue stepped forward six inches in perfect unison.

Elena stood at position number forty-seven. In her pocket, her phone vibrated with a gentle, encouraging reminder: *“Your Certainty Index will reach 60.0% upon check-in.”*

She looked at the boy ahead of her. He was nineteen, with freshly cut hair and polished shoes. He was smiling with the peaceful, untroubled expression of someone who had just been granted permission to exist without effort for the next fifty years.

Elena looked past him, through the tall glass doors of the pavilion. Inside, rows of clerks sat behind double-pane glass, stamping vouchers with rhythmic, muffled thuds. The light inside was warm, yellow, and completely still.

She felt the blue voucher in her fingers. It felt smooth, sterile, and cold.

The bell chimed again. The boy ahead of her took a step forward.

Elena did not.

She stepped to the left, unhooked the velvet guide rope, and ducked beneath it.

A supervisor with a clipboard looked up, his brow furrowing with mild, benevolent concern. "Miss? The entrance is straight ahead. You’re losing your position in the sequence."

"I know," Elena said.

She did not run. Running would look like panic, and she was not panicked. She turned her back on the brass doors, walked across the wet stone plaza, and headed down the narrow, unpaved alleyway where the old delivery vans were parked and the smell of hot metal and diesel hung heavy in the damp air.

Her phone chimed in her pocket as the Certainty Index began to recalculate, dropping into the dark, uncharted territory of zero. For the first time in twenty-two years, she felt entirely awake.
