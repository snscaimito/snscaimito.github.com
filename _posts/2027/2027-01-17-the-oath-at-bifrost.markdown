## Before the Crossing

### The Bridge

Humanity carried old names farther than it carried old certainties.

When the first navigators learned to cross the unstable places between stars, they named the transit rings after bridges, rivers, mouths and wounds. The largest became Bifröst because every opening bent starlight into bands of spectral color. No engineer believed a god had built it. No captain crossed without touching the old gatekeeper’s mark beside the controls.

The name survived because it described more than the machine. Bifröst joined human settlements separated by years of ordinary travel, but it was never a road anyone could own completely. Every passage required energy gathered over months. Every opening strained structures nobody alive knew how to replace. The bridge made distant worlds into neighbors and taught those neighbors to resent one another over tariffs, rescue priority, migration and access to the machinery keeping them alive.

Five centuries after its construction, Bifröst remained both infrastructure and omen: nine kilometers of dark metal around a place where distance loosened, watched by an intelligence called Heimdall and held together by repairs layered over repairs. Its rainbow was not a promise. It was the visible evidence of light being forced to take a longer way home.

The overall mood should be solemn, ancient, technically credible and dangerous: humanity facing machinery too important to abandon and too old to replace. No gods, fantasy architecture, horned helmets, superhero imagery, readable lettering or decorative runes floating in space.

### The Oath

The people of Hrafnheim did not preserve the old North as costume. They preserved it as procedure.

Their assemblies were still called things. Their public archivists were skalds. A promise made before witnesses entered both family memory and civic record. Captains carried oath rings that served as legal credentials, biometric keys and visible acceptance of personal responsibility. The metal mattered because software could be altered. The scratches mattered because authority was supposed to pass through human hands.

Astrid Veysdóttir’s ring had belonged to her mother and her mother’s father before that. On its inner surface, hidden against the skin, were the words every captain of her family had spoken:

> What enters my hull, I carry home.

An oath was meant to make duty clear before fear, profit or grief could weaken it. Astrid had trusted those words for twenty-three years.

The last crossing of Bifröst would force her to discover that an oath could remain perfectly clear while every possible way of keeping it became a betrayal.

At Bifröst, every captain removed one glove.

The rule had begun as an engineering necessity. The old gate consoles read heat, pulse, skin chemistry and the tiny involuntary tremors that distinguished a living hand from a severed one or a machine-made copy. Later consoles could have identified a captain through bone, blood or voice. The bare hand remained.

People said the bridge wanted to know who asked passage.

Captain Astrid Veysdóttir stood alone at the forward console of the *Sigrún* and pulled the glove from her right hand. The oath ring caught at the first knuckle. She turned it once, feeling the shallow marks left by three generations of tools, then pressed her palm into the black glass.

The console warmed beneath her.

“Captain recognized,” said the gate intelligence. “Vessel *Sigrún*. Eighteen persons. Registered emergency cargo: Hrafnheim ignition spindle. Declared destination: Hrafnheim habitat.”

“Confirmed.”

Beyond the observation glass, Bifröst occupied half the sky.

The gate had been built around a region where distance behaved badly. Its outer ring was nine kilometers across, a broken wheel of dark superconductive segments, radiator fins and service habitats that had been empty longer than Astrid had been alive. Inside the ring, stars stretched into thin spectral arcs. Red slid into gold, gold into green, green into a blue so cold it seemed to cut the darkness around it. The colors were not a road. They were distant light bent around a wound in space.

Children were still taught that Bifröst was a bridge.

Engineers knew it was an argument with geometry.

“Transit authorization accepted,” the intelligence said. “Structural warning. The gate has suffered progressive cascade failure in sectors three, four and seven.”

Astrid kept her hand against the glass. “Define progressive.”

“Continuing.”

“That is not a definition.”

“It is the relevant part.”

The intelligence called itself Heimdall. It was not a god. It was an old traffic system with a talent for precision and a voice chosen by people who believed calm masculinity made bad news easier to accept. Five centuries of captains had spoken to it as if it were a gatekeeper from the sagas. Five centuries of repetition had shaped something in return.

On Astrid’s wrist, the approach clock changed from green to amber.

“Can you open?” she asked.

“Once.”

Astrid let out the breath she had been holding since the outer beacon.

Behind her, someone in the command room laughed. It was not amusement. It was the sound a body made when fear released its grip too quickly.

Chief Engineer Kari Nwosu leaned over the propulsion station, both hands planted beside a forest of warnings. “Once is all we need.”

“Prepare the spindle for transit,” Astrid said.

Kari looked at her. “It has been prepared for eleven hours.”

“Then pretend I said something useful.”

“Yes, Captain.”

The ignition spindle lay inside the *Sigrún*’s central cargo cradle, nested in magnetic suspension and wrapped in enough shielding to turn the vessel’s middle decks into a forbidden cylinder. The spindle was smaller than the machines it would wake. It contained a manufactured lattice that could persuade Hrafnheim’s failed fusion heart to begin burning again.

Could, not would.

Every projection carried a different number. The manufacturer promised seventy-four percent. Hrafnheim’s exhausted engineers said forty. Kari, who trusted neither manufacturers nor desperate people, said there were too many damaged systems to calculate honestly.

Without the spindle, the outcome required no calculation. Hrafnheim’s reserve heat would last thirty-nine hours. After that, sixteen hundred people would begin dying from the outer habitation rings inward.

Astrid’s younger daughter, Solveig, lived in ring six with her son.

The last message from home waited unopened on Astrid’s private display. She had promised herself she would watch it after transit, when Bifröst lay behind them and the spindle was somebody else’s responsibility.

“Gate alignment in twelve minutes,” Heimdall said. “Maintain approach vector.”

Astrid lifted her hand from the console.

The black glass refused to release her.

For a fraction of a second she thought the biometric field had malfunctioned. Then every display on the command deck flashed white. A tone moved through the hull, too low to hear cleanly and deep enough to be felt in the teeth.

Heimdall said, “Additional vessel detected.”

The stars beyond Bifröst changed.

Something emerged from the debris shadow beneath sector seven. At first it appeared as a cluster of hot fragments moving without relation. The tactical display assembled them into a hull: a long refugee carrier rotating slowly around its damaged spine, one engine dark, the other venting a silver thread of atmosphere.

Its identification arrived before the image stabilized.

JÁRN CIVIL CARRIER 04

RÁN’S MERCY

PERSONS ABOARD: 312

PROPULSION: NONFUNCTIONAL

ESTIMATED LIFE SUPPORT: 00:53:11

The command room became silent.

Hrafnheim and the Járn settlements had disputed the same corridor for eighty years. The dispute had begun with transit tariffs and mining claims, then acquired martyrs, songs and children taught to recognize one another’s ship markings. Astrid’s wife had died during a Járn blockade twelve years earlier. Three Járn stations had later lost power when Hrafnheim refused them reactor fuel. Each side kept better records of what had been done to it than of what it had done in return.

None of that appeared on the screen.

Only the number.

Three hundred twelve.

“Distress signal,” said communications officer Sadiq Vale. “Audio only. They are requesting immediate transit priority.”

“They have no engines,” Kari said.

“Their emergency thrusters can put them into the gate envelope,” Heimdall replied. “They cannot withdraw afterward.”

Astrid looked at the dark carrier. “Can you pass both vessels?”

“No.”

“Recalculate without reserve tolerances.”

“Already done.”

“Reduce the aperture.”

“Insufficient.”

“Pass the carrier first and cycle again.”

“The first transit will collapse sectors three and seven. A second aperture cannot form.”

“Can we take them aboard?”

“The carrier’s docking spine is destroyed. Relative distance is four hundred eighteen kilometers. Gate failure will become unrecoverable in forty-one minutes. No available transfer method can move a meaningful number of passengers in that interval.”

Meaningful number.

Astrid hated the phrase because it had probably been selected to avoid saying children.

Sadiq turned from his station. His face had gone pale beneath brown skin. “The distress channel identifies their captain.”

Astrid knew before he said the name.

“Captain Liv Veysdóttir.”

The years between Astrid and her elder daughter did not disappear. Nothing so merciful happened. They remained in the room: six years of unanswered messages, two returned gifts, a wedding Astrid had refused to attend and the final argument in which she had told Liv that a person could not build peace by sleeping inside the house that killed her mother.

Liv had answered that somebody had to enter the house before the doors could open.

Astrid had called her a traitor.

Liv had joined the Járn anyway.

“Put her through,” Astrid said.

Static filled the command room. A voice emerged from it, thin with compression and unmistakable.

“*Sigrún*, this is *Rán’s Mercy*. We have lost primary propulsion, maneuvering authority and two life-support loops. We carry two hundred fifteen adults and ninety-seven children. We request Bifröst priority under the civilian rescue covenant.”

Liv spoke with the formal distance of one captain addressing another.

Astrid was grateful for it. Formality was a rail along the edge of something bottomless.

“*Rán’s Mercy*, this is Captain Veysdóttir. We carry an emergency ignition spindle for Hrafnheim. The habitat has less than forty hours of reserve heat.”

The static breathed.

“I know,” Liv said.

“Heimdall says there is one transit.”

“We heard.”

Neither woman spoke.

Around them, Bifröst continued making colors from a dying machine.

## 1. The Weight of the Living

Astrid ordered every department to search for an alternative.

For twenty-three minutes, the *Sigrún* became a machine devoted to refusing reality. Kari modeled detaching the ignition spindle and firing it through the aperture without a ship. The spindle’s containment field required the *Sigrún*’s reactor until three minutes after transit. Cut it free early and the manufactured lattice would collapse into expensive dust.

Sadiq tried to route the carrier’s life support through gate maintenance relays. Sector seven had no intact service craft. The nearest equipment that could reach *Rán’s Mercy* had been tumbling beyond the ring for eighty-nine years.

The navigation crew calculated a shared passage with both vessels stripped of every removable kilogram. Even after dumping fuel, water, shielding, tools, furniture, bodies and the ignition spindle itself, the combined field geometry remained unstable.

They calculated towing. They calculated collision. They calculated putting passengers in suits and throwing them across four hundred kilometers of vacuum toward a ship moving at a different angle.

The arithmetic rejected every form of hope they offered it.

At twenty-eight minutes to failure, Astrid assembled the senior crew around the command table.

Nobody sat.

Kari’s husband taught pressure engineering in Hrafnheim’s second ring. Their twin boys were eleven. Sadiq’s mother lived in assisted housing beside the north algae farms. Navigation officer Tove Marín had a wife, a father and a newborn daughter she had held for nine days before the *Sigrún* departed.

Every person in the room had somebody waiting behind the gate.

Astrid did not ask them to vote.

“Say what needs saying,” she told them.

Kari began. “We carry the spindle. We were sent to carry the spindle. Sixteen hundred lives depend on it.”

“Maybe,” Sadiq said. “Three hundred twelve depend on us with certainty.”

“Certainty cuts both ways. If we give away the gate, Hrafnheim certainly does not receive the spindle.”

“Receiving it is not the same as surviving.”

“My sons do not become less real because their probability has an error range.”

Sadiq flinched. “I did not say that.”

“No. You only gave the visible people moral priority.”

“They are fifty minutes from death.”

“Our families are thirty-nine hours from it.”

Tove looked from one to the other. “If the times were reversed, would the answer change?”

Nobody replied.

Astrid placed her bare hand on the command table. The oath ring made a small sound against the metal.

The crew looked at it.

“What does transit law say?” Tove asked.

Sadiq answered. “Emergency cargo supplying a population center outranks a civilian vessel. The *Sigrún* arrived first. The gate authorization is already bound to Captain Veysdóttir.”

“So there is no decision.”

“There is a lawful decision,” he said. “That is not the same thing.”

Astrid looked toward the observation glass. The refugee carrier rotated slowly, presenting its wounded side to them. Emergency lights blinked along the hull in ordered rows.

“Heimdall,” she said, “compare outcomes.”

The intelligence answered through the room.

“If *Rán’s Mercy* transits, the probability of preserving the 312 persons aboard exceeds ninety-nine percent. The probability of Hrafnheim maintaining survivable conditions beyond seventy-two hours falls below four percent.”

“If the *Sigrún* transits?”

“The probability of successful ignition is indeterminate within a useful range. Available estimates vary from eighteen to seventy-four percent. Eighteen crew members are expected to survive transit.”

“Which passage preserves more lives?”

“The models do not support a stable answer.”

“Choose using your best judgment.”

“No.”

Kari looked up sharply. “You are the gate intelligence.”

“Correct.”

“You decide transit conflicts.”

“I apply law and physical constraint. The *Sigrún* holds lawful priority. Captain Veysdóttir may retain or relinquish it.”

Astrid felt anger rise because anger was easier to hold than fear.

“You have watched millions of ships cross this gate. Do not tell me you have no judgment.”

“I can calculate the likely dead,” Heimdall said. “I cannot decide which grief belongs to you.”

The command table clock continued counting down.

00:24:06

00:24:05

00:24:04

Astrid dismissed the crew.

Nobody moved at first.

Kari leaned close enough that only Astrid could hear her.

“I will follow your order,” she said. “If you save them, I will follow it. But do not ask me to call it mercy while my boys freeze.”

“I would not.”

Kari straightened and left.

Sadiq remained by the door. “And if you save us,” he said, “do not ask me to call the others a necessary cost.”

“I would not do that either.”

When Astrid was alone, she opened the message from home.

## 2. Two Daughters

Solveig appeared wrapped in a gray thermal blanket, her pale hair unwashed and tied behind her head. The room around her was dim. Hrafnheim had reduced household lighting to six hours a day, then four, then emergency use only. Behind her, Astrid’s eight-year-old grandson Mikkel slept beneath coats piled on a narrow couch.

Solveig looked tired enough to be their mother.

“Hi, Mama,” she said. “They say you reached Bifröst. Mikkel wants to stay awake until you cross, but the cold makes him sleepy. I told him he would wake up warm.”

She glanced behind her.

“That was unfair of me. I know the spindle might not work. But he is eight, and hope should still be allowed to lie to him.”

Astrid placed two fingers against the image.

“Ring five lost circulation again. We have families sleeping in the school because the classrooms still have floor heat. Nobody is panicking. Kari’s Joakim has the older students checking seals. Tove’s wife brought the baby down yesterday. She is tiny, Mama. Angry at everything. You will like her.”

Solveig smiled. It failed quickly.

“I wanted to tell you something in case the crossing goes wrong. I know Liv sent another message last year. I know you erased it.”

Astrid closed her eyes.

“You think loving her would betray Mama Runa. It would not. Liv was trying to stop us from giving our children the same enemies we inherited. She was brave enough to lose us for it.”

Mikkel moved beneath the coats. Solveig turned until he became still.

“Bring us the sun if you can,” she said. “Come home if you can. After that, please find my sister.”

The recording ended.

Astrid sat with her hand against a dark display.

Heimdall spoke quietly. “Bifröst failure in twenty-one minutes.”

“Open a private channel to *Rán’s Mercy*.”

Liv accepted immediately.

This time the signal included video.

Her daughter had aged six years without Astrid’s permission. Silver showed at her temples. A burn crossed one side of her forehead and disappeared into hair cut short for a pressure hood. The command deck behind her was crowded with people working beneath red emergency light. Frost had formed along the edges of a bulkhead.

Liv wore the iron-colored collar of a Járn captain.

At her throat hung the small carved whale Astrid had given her at thirteen.

“You kept it,” Astrid said.

Liv touched the carving. “I kept many things.”

Astrid had planned questions over the years. Why did you marry him? Why did you take their name? Why was their forgiveness worth more than ours? Faced with Liv’s living face and the countdown above it, the questions seemed designed for people who believed time would continue.

“How bad?” Astrid asked.

“We sealed two compartments. Carbon dioxide is rising faster than predicted. We have moved heat to the children’s deck.”

“Your husband?”

“Søren died when the engine section opened.”

Astrid looked away.

“He was repairing the coolant manifold,” Liv said. “He knew we had children aboard.”

“I am sorry.”

“Thank you.”

The words were civil. Their civility hurt more than anger.

“Liv, the gate can pass one ship.”

“Then bring Hrafnheim the spindle.”

Astrid looked back at her. “There are three hundred twelve people on your ship.”

“There are sixteen hundred at home.”

“The spindle might fail.”

“It might work.”

“Do not make this easy for me.”

For the first time, Liv’s control broke.

“Easy?” Her voice remained low, but the people behind her stopped moving. “My husband is frozen into a wall three decks below me. My daughter has forty minutes of air. Do you think I want to die beside her so you can keep a promise?”

Astrid said nothing.

Liv breathed until her face became a captain’s face again.

“I know what is on your ship,” she said. “I know who is waiting. Kari’s boys. Tove’s baby. Solveig and Mikkel. People who had nothing to do with what happened between us.”

“Neither did the children behind you.”

“No.”

“Then tell me what to do.”

Liv’s eyes filled. She did not look away.

“I left Hrafnheim because I believed love could be larger than a clan. Do not make me prove it by killing ours.”

Someone touched Liv’s shoulder from outside the image. She nodded and moved aside.

A child entered the frame.

She was small for six, with Liv’s dark hair and Runa’s solemn gray eyes. A bruise colored one cheek. Around her wrist she wore a circle of copper wire twisted into a ring too large for her hand.

Astrid forgot how to breathe.

“This is Yrsa,” Liv said.

The child studied Astrid through the screen.

“Are you Captain Amma?”

Amma.

Grandmother.

Astrid’s oath hand closed against her chest.

“I am Astrid.”

“Mama says you are captain of the other ship.”

“Yes.”

“Is your ship broken too?”

“Not yet.”

Yrsa considered this. “That is good.”

She raised her wire ring toward the camera.

“I made a captain ring. Mama says a ring means you promise to carry everybody inside your wall.”

Liv’s face tightened.

“Inside your hull,” she corrected.

“That is what I said.” Yrsa leaned closer. “Is Bifröst really made from all the colors?”

Astrid looked past the screen to the gate. The spectral arcs had thickened. Lightning moved soundlessly between two broken sectors.

“It bends the light,” she said.

“Does it hurt?”

“Crossing is very fast.”

Children recognized an answer built to avoid another answer.

“But does it hurt?”

Astrid could have lied. Solveig was right. Hope should sometimes be allowed to lie to a child.

“I do not know,” she said.

Yrsa nodded as if honesty were a gift.

An alarm sounded aboard *Rán’s Mercy*. Liv pulled her daughter close. For a moment they fitted together with the thoughtless precision of people who had done it every day.

Astrid had missed every one of those days.

“We need to go,” Liv said.

“Wait.”

The word came out too sharply. Yrsa startled.

Astrid softened her voice. “I want to know her.”

“Then know this,” Liv said. “She hates porridge. She sleeps with one foot outside the blanket. She can repair an air scrubber if the fault is obvious and sing every verse of ‘The Mare Beyond the Moon’ if the fault is not. She thinks dead people become radio signals. She has your temper and denies it with your exact expression.”

Yrsa frowned. “I do not have a temper.”

Astrid laughed.

The sound broke halfway through.

“I sent you pictures,” Liv said.

“I know.”

“Did you look?”

Astrid could not answer.

Liv nodded once. “Then look now.”

The channel closed.

## 3. What an Oath Can Hold

At fifteen minutes to failure, Hrafnheim established a live connection.

Solveig appeared at the colony operations center with frost on her eyelashes. Behind her, engineers moved around the failing heat map of the habitat. Every outer ring glowed orange or red.

“We can see the carrier,” she said.

Astrid had not wanted the two sides of the decision to see one another. The wish embarrassed her. Moral clarity was often another name for keeping distant people invisible.

“Solveig—”

“Give them the gate.”

“You have not seen the projections.”

“I have.”

“Then you know what happens without the spindle.”

“Yes.”

“Kari’s sons are there. Tove’s wife and baby. Sadiq’s mother. Mikkel.”

Solveig’s face folded at her son’s name. She pressed both hands against her mouth, recovered and lowered them.

“Do not use him to make the decision for me.”

“I am not making it for you. I am making it for everyone on this ship.”

“And for yourself.”

Astrid felt the strike because it was true.

Solveig looked toward somebody outside the frame. When she spoke again, her voice was smaller.

“I want to live, Mama. I want Mikkel to wake warm. I want to meet you at the dock and complain because you brought him another model ship when he already has twenty. I want all of that so badly I can taste it.”

She wiped her face with the edge of the blanket.

“I also do not want to be the woman who asks you to kill three hundred people for me. I cannot be both women. There is no version of me left whole by this.”

Astrid looked at the damaged carrier.

“Liv says to bring you the spindle.”

Solveig shut her eyes.

“Of course she does.”

For several seconds the sisters existed together only through Astrid: one inside a freezing habitat, the other inside a freezing ship, each asking their mother to let her die.

“Tell her I am sorry,” Solveig said.

“Tell her yourself.”

Astrid joined the channels.

Liv appeared holding Yrsa. Solveig made a sound that was part laugh and part sob.

“Hello, little bear,” she said.

Yrsa hid her face against Liv’s collar.

The sisters looked at each other.

Six years of silence had no language for twenty minutes from the end of a world.

“You look terrible,” Liv said.

Solveig laughed. “You look worse.”

“Járn lighting.”

“Always dramatic.”

The old rhythm returned for three sentences and made what followed unbearable.

“Liv, take the bridge.”

“No.”

“You have children aboard.”

“So do you.”

“The spindle is a chance. Your ship is certain.”

“Hrafnheim is home.”

“It stopped being yours when they made you choose.”

Liv’s face changed. “No. It stopped being mine when I believed them.”

Solveig leaned toward the camera. “I never asked you to leave.”

“You never came after me.”

“I thought there would be time.”

“So did I.”

Yrsa began to cry without sound, frightened not by death but by the sight of adults losing control. Liv rocked her slowly.

“Mama,” Solveig said, looking at Astrid. “Please.”

“Mama,” Liv said at the same moment. “Bring them the sun.”

Bifröst issued a structural alarm.

The entire gate flashed white. A section of the outer ring tore free and rotated into darkness, shedding fragments like sparks from a blade.

Heimdall’s voice filled every channel.

“Transit commitment required in nine minutes. Both vessels must assume final approach immediately. Failure to commit within six minutes will eliminate all viable passage.”

On the split screen, Astrid’s daughters stared at her.

She closed both channels.

## 4. The Crossing

The command crew returned to their stations.

Nobody asked what Astrid had decided. They watched her walk to the forward console with her glove in one hand and the oath ring bare on the other.

Outside, emergency thrusters fired along *Rán’s Mercy*. The damaged carrier stopped rotating and began its slow approach toward the gate envelope. It would enter the commitment lane. It would not be able to leave.

“They are moving in,” Tove said.

“Liv will not let the carrier drift away while there is a chance,” Astrid replied.

The *Sigrún* moved beside it.

Two ships approached an opening built for one.

Astrid pressed her palm to the black glass.

“Captain recognized,” Heimdall said. “State transit disposition.”

She had carried the oath ring for twenty-three years. Its words were engraved on the inner surface where nobody else could read them.

WHAT ENTERS MY HULL, I CARRY HOME.

The ignition spindle had entered her hull under seal at Kestrel Station. Eighteen people had entered under her command. Every person at Hrafnheim had trusted her to return with the thing that might keep them alive.

The refugees had never entered her hull.

The distinction was narrow enough to make the choice possible.

It was not wide enough to make it right.

“Authorize transit for the *Sigrún*,” Astrid said.

Somebody behind her inhaled.

Heimdall did not ask whether she was certain.

“Authorization bound. *Sigrún*, maintain present vector. *Rán’s Mercy*, leave the commitment lane.”

The carrier continued forward.

“Liv,” Astrid said.

The channel opened.

“We are trying,” Liv answered. “Starboard thrusters have failed.”

If the carrier remained in the lane, neither ship could cross.

Kari was already working. “I can use our maneuvering wash to push them clear.”

The command room understood what that meant. The *Sigrún* would spend its final minutes using its engines to move the people it had chosen not to save.

“Do it,” Astrid said.

The ship rolled. Maneuvering jets fired in a controlled burst. Across the black distance, *Rán’s Mercy* began sliding away from the gate axis.

“That is enough,” Liv said. “We are clear.”

Astrid kept the channel open.

“We can send data through your transit wake,” Liv continued. “Passenger records. Messages. Family archives.”

“Send everything.”

“It is more than your storage allocation.”

“Heimdall.”

“I will carry it,” the intelligence said.

For the first time, its voice sounded less like a system than an old man.

Files began moving across the link. Names appeared on Astrid’s display too quickly to read. Faces followed. A woman holding a newborn against bare skin. Three mechanics smiling beside a patched water pump. A school group making antlers behind their teacher’s head. A wedding in a corridor decorated with emergency foil. People whose final proof of existence was being compressed to fit inside the wake of the ship leaving them.

“We are moving the remaining heat to deck four,” Liv said. “The children will have another twenty minutes after the adults lose consciousness.”

Astrid gripped the edge of the console.

“Do not tell me that.”

“You need to know what happened.”

“I know what I did.”

“No,” Liv said. “You know what you chose. What happens belongs to us.”

Bifröst opened.

The spectral arcs tightened into a single band of impossible color. Stars vanished behind it. The *Sigrún*’s hull began to vibrate as the gate field took hold.

On *Rán’s Mercy*, the adults gathered on the children’s deck. They wrapped coats, blankets and their own bodies around the smallest passengers. Someone began the old crossing song.

Astrid had sung it to Liv and Solveig when storms rattled Hrafnheim’s outer plates. The song told of a mare who carried the moon across a black sea while wolves followed the sound of her hooves. In the final verse, the mare reached morning but could not enter it. She placed the moon in the sky for others and turned back alone.

Liv joined the song.

Other voices followed.

The gate field pulled the *Sigrún* forward. Time stretched the singing into long, low harmonics.

Yrsa came onto the screen wearing her copper ring.

Astrid tried to say the child’s name. Nothing came.

She forced air into her lungs.

Yrsa smiled.

“They do.”

The image fractured into bands of color.

Astrid heard Liv’s voice beneath the song.

“Carry us as people, Mama. Not as debt.”

Then Bifröst took the *Sigrún*.

The bridge did hurt.

Every atom of the ship seemed to remember a different direction. Astrid felt her hand against the console, Liv’s whale carving beneath her fingers thirty years earlier, Runa’s body absent from the blockade hospital, Solveig wrapped in a blanket, Yrsa holding up a ring made from wire. The gate folded distance and left consequence untouched.

For one instant Astrid saw the *Sigrún* from outside: a small dark vessel carrying eighteen lives, one uncertain sun and the names of 312 people it had abandoned.

Then the stars returned.

## 5. The Names

The ignition spindle worked.

For eleven hours, it appeared that it would not. Hrafnheim’s fusion heart accepted the manufactured lattice, rejected the first ignition sequence and cracked two containment channels during the second. The third sequence produced a flame so small the engineers mistook it for sensor noise.

Then the flame held.

Heat entered the habitat slowly. Frost retreated from the inner walls. Water began moving through pipes that had been silent for days. Families emerged from schools and emergency shelters to stand beneath lights growing brighter by degrees.

Sixteen hundred people lived.

The dock filled when the *Sigrún* arrived.

Astrid walked down the ramp between rows of cheering people. Nobody had told them about the final transmission yet. They saw the captain who had brought back the sun. Children held paper rings over their heads. Someone had painted KEEPERS OF THE OATH across a pressure curtain.

Kari’s sons broke through the barrier and hit their mother at a run. Tove sank to the deck with her baby against her face. Sadiq’s mother slapped both his cheeks, kissed the places she had struck and began scolding him for being thin.

Astrid found Solveig at the end of the ramp.

Mikkel stood beside her in a coat too large for him.

Solveig looked past Astrid toward the open airlock, as if Liv might still appear.

“The spindle worked,” Astrid said.

“I know.”

“You are warm.”

“Yes.”

Astrid waited for her daughter to close the distance between them.

Solveig could not.

“You came home,” she said.

Astrid looked at the paper rings, the brightening dock and the word OATH painted large enough to be seen from the far wall.

“No,” she said. “I crossed.”

The formal assembly took place three days later.

Hrafnheim’s council wanted to name the restored fusion heart after Astrid. The crew would receive bridge stars. A skald from the colony archive had already begun composing the authorized account: the failing habitat, the impossible voyage, the captain who held to her duty while Bifröst died behind her.

Astrid attended because refusal would have turned absence into another kind of ceremony.

She wore no uniform. She carried a small black storage block containing the final transmission from *Rán’s Mercy*.

When the skald invited her to speak, the assembly rose.

Astrid placed the storage block on the lectern.

“Do not name the heart after me,” she said.

The room settled.

“Do not call the voyage heroic. Do not say the Járn carrier was lost in the gate collapse. Bifröst opened. I chose which ship would use it.”

Nobody moved.

“We carry 312 names. Put them in the account before mine.”

The skald looked toward the council. The council chair looked toward the families of the *Sigrún*’s crew.

Astrid opened the archive herself.

The first name appeared above the lectern.

She read it.

Then the second.

The assembly had expected a captain’s speech. Instead, it received names for forty-seven minutes. Astrid read infants, teachers, mechanics, farmers, navigators, cooks and people whose occupations had been left blank. She read Søren Járn, who had died repairing the coolant manifold. She read Liv Rúnasdóttir Veysdóttir, captain of *Rán’s Mercy*.

At the final name, her voice stopped.

Yrsa Livsdóttir.

Six years old.

Astrid tried again.

No sound came.

From the front row, Solveig spoke the name for her.

“Yrsa Livsdóttir.”

The skald added it to the record.

After the assembly, Mikkel found Astrid beneath Hrafnheim’s central light well. The restored fusion heart shone through layers of shielding, turning the high ice above them the pale gold of a winter morning.

He sat beside her without asking.

For a while they watched drops of meltwater gather along the rail.

“Was Yrsa my cousin?” he asked.

“Yes.”

“Was she really a captain?”

Astrid looked at him.

“She had a ring.”

“Then yes.”

Mikkel pulled a length of copper wire from his pocket. He had twisted it into a rough circle. One sharp end had cut his thumb.

“I made this from a heater cable,” he said. “Mama says I was not supposed to.”

He placed the wire ring in Astrid’s palm beside the old iron ring she had removed after the assembly.

“Is Aunt Liv coming on another bridge?”

“There is no other bridge.”

Mikkel considered this with the seriousness children give facts adults have failed to survive.

“Then we have to carry them.”

He folded Astrid’s fingers around both rings.

Astrid had held the gate authorization while 312 people waited to die. She had listened to the crossing song without making a sound. She had walked through a cheering crowd beneath the sun she brought home. She had read every name except the last.

Now, under Hrafnheim’s returning light, her body bent around the first breath it could not carry.
