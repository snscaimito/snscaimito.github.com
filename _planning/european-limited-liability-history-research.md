# European limited liability: research map for an X series

Working notes, September 25, 2026. This is a research document, not publication copy. `_planning/` is excluded from the Jekyll build in `_config.yml`.

## The question that holds the series together

Why can a founder often form a US LLC or corporation with little capital, while a European GmbH, S.L., S.r.l., or SARL may involve a notary, paid-in capital, and several steps? Follow the answer through time from the founder's point of view. At each stop, make the alternative tangible: trading personally or through an unlimited-liability partnership can put the entrepreneur's own assets behind business debts. Do not suggest that incorporation protects against every debt, guarantee, or wrongful act.

The defensible historical frame is a bargain over **access to limited liability and protection for creditors**. The continental private limited company often *widened* access compared with older corporate forms. Minimum capital, formal deeds, and public registration made that access conditional. The claim that nobility designed these particular forms to exclude ordinary founders is not established by the sources gathered so far; do not use it as the series' factual premise.

Each X post must stand on its own: establish the place and date naturally within the account, then show the actor, personal-liability problem, rule or reform, and consequence for that actor. A sequence number may orient readers but cannot carry necessary context. Images should depict the specific time and place; avoid anachronistic offices, money, clothing, and documents. Do not attribute invented words to a real historical person.

Series workflow: a proposed post exists in the current task conversation until the user asks to queue it. Do not leave finished copy in a separate draft, review, held, or pending state outside the queue. When asked to queue, save the canonical story and matching image, create a card with `status: queued`, and verify that its preview works and the series cadence lists it. Queuing is not authorization to publish on X.

**Writing guideline for X:** Put the hook within the first 280 characters of the canonical post text. Open with the actor's immediate jeopardy, a sharp legal contrast, or a decision with visible stakes; give enough place, date, and context that the preview makes sense on its own. The hook must be accurate and earned by the rest of the account. Keep the full story intact; the 280-character preview is not a post-length limit.

Always tell each episode as a first-person account by an actor of its time, with enough context for a reader in 2026 who does not know how these company forms began. Let the narrator encounter the filing, capital, creditor, or wage rule through an event, document, conversation, or inner thought. Let inner thoughts carry needed explanation without breaking the scene. Give only the context needed to understand the predicament. Do not use textbook paragraphs, retrospective lectures, or an explanatory moral at the end.

**Image guideline for suspense:** When a story turns on a choice between limited liability and personal exposure, use a photorealistic split view when it clarifies that choice. Put the immediate opportunity or legal obstacle on one side and the household or livelihood at risk on the other, with one actor connecting the halves. Build tension through a visible decision or approaching threat; do not show an invented outcome as a documented event. Keep the setting, clothing, tools, documents, and light plausible for the stated place and year. Avoid text overlays, modern props, and imagery that presents a reconstruction as an actual period photograph.

## Where today's friction is greatest

No single country is “most restrictive”: waiting time, locked-up capital, fees, and compulsory intermediaries measure different things. The World Bank's 2025 B-READY *Business Entry* measure covers steps from before through after registration for a standardized limited-company scenario. It is not the registry's processing time, and its 2025 country reports do not cover every EU member.

Important counterexamples: [Slovenia says](https://spot.gov.si/en/info/company-registration/limited-liability-company-d-o-o) a simple d.o.o. can be registered within a few days once its conditions are met, despite the World Bank's 30-day end-to-end figure. [Portugal's Empresa na Hora](https://registo.justica.gov.pt/Empresas/Empresa-na-Hora) can constitute and register a company at one counter in one visit, though other steps can follow. These distinctions must appear whenever we use the longer figures.

## U.S. counter-journey: how permission gave way to filing

The United States is the comparison in the opening question, but there is no single U.S. company law. States create corporations and LLCs; federal tax law classifies them separately. The central shift was from a **special legislative charter for a particular business** toward **incorporation under a general law by filing a certificate**. That shift happened unevenly. It did not instantly remove restrictions on purpose, number of founders, shareholder exposure, or operating licenses. [Eric Hilt's state-by-state study](https://www.nber.org/papers/w21195) finds that some general laws were restrictive and that some states without them granted many individual charters. Do not equate “general law” with universal access or say all U.S. founders had an easy route from 1811 onward.

**Comparative story angle:** In 1837 Lyon, the narrator's SA still waits for a royal authorization; across the Atlantic a qualifying New York manufacturer can already use a general filing law. The sharper question is *who qualifies for the filing route, and who is still personally answerable when a company fails?* A later pair can juxtapose the 1892 GmbH's capital and deed with the 1977 LLC's tax-driven invention, then show a current Delaware founder whose low entry capital does not erase filing costs, annual tax, licenses, or a creditor's demand for a personal signature. Each eventual U.S. post must explain its own place, year, entity, stakes, and outcome without requiring the European episode as context.

**Published counterpoint: New York, 1848, in two standalone posts.** The [coal supplier scene](company-law-in-europe/new-york-1848-pigment-coal-supplier.md) and [matching 2:1 split image](european-limited-liability-images/new-york-1848-pigment-coal-supplier-split.png) show the founder's personal horse beyond an ordinary trade creditor's reach after the founders have fully paid their capital and recorded the required certificate. The [workers' wages scene](company-law-in-europe/new-york-1848-pigment-workers-wages.md) and [matching 2:1 split image](european-limited-liability-images/new-york-1848-pigment-workers-wages-split.png) show that same horse sold voluntarily to pay a different debt: section 18 made shareholders personally liable for qualifying laborers' wages. These are two situations involving the same fictional pigment works in 1848, each a first-person account that can stand alone. They were published as `company-law-in-europe-06` (coal merchant) and `company-law-in-europe-07` (founder and workers), after the French noble-family post. Both images are fictional reconstructions, not period photographs. A [contemporary printing of the 1848 act](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/3/31/Report_of_the_committee_on_establishing_a_general_smelting_%26_refining_company_in_the_city_of_New_York_%28IA_reportofcommitte00unse_9%29.pdf) gives three or more founders in manufacturing, mining, mechanical, or chemical work a certificate-filing route through the county clerk and secretary of state. Sections 10–11 made ordinary shareholder exposure contingent on full payment of capital and the separate recorded certificate; the [U.S. Supreme Court later quoted those sections](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep109/usrep109371/usrep109371.pdf). The [New York Court of Appeals quoted and applied the original wage rule](https://openjurist.org/52/ny/203/johnson-v-underhill).

## Questions to resolve before drafting a post

- For each historical scene, obtain the law **as it stood then**, legislative explanation where possible, and evidence for the alternative unlimited-liability form. Present-day rules cannot be projected backward.
- Separate **nominal capital**, **cash paid before registration**, **fees**, and **money still usable by the company**. Capital is not simply a government fee.
- Distinguish **legal liability of the company** from founders' personal guarantees, pre-incorporation obligations, director liability, fraud, and special statutory exceptions.
- Verify whether the chosen founder could use a simplified form, online registration, or a one-stop service at that date.
- Treat isolated reports of six-week or three-month formations as individual experiences, never a national average.
- For U.S. scenes, distinguish **state permission or filing**, **shareholder/member liability**, **federal tax classification**, and **permission to operate**. Do not project today's single-member LLC or Delaware process backward onto New York in 1811 or Wyoming in 1977.

## Series shape

Start with the personal-liability dilemma; travel through Germany, Austria, France, Italy, and Spain; then show today's surviving rules and reforms through Luxembourg and Sweden. Place U.S. episodes at their own historical turning points as counterpoints: the charter seeker, the qualifying New York manufacturer, the Wyoming LLC proponent, and the present-day Delaware founder. Each standalone episode should close on one precise unresolved tension: who should bear the loss when a small company fails—the founder, the creditor, or someone else?

## Published first episode: Germany, May 1892

The [first-person story](company-law-in-europe/germany-1892.md) and its [matching image](european-limited-liability-images/germany-1892-family-workshop.png) were [published on X on September 25, 2026](https://x.com/snscaimito/status/2103513166596506067) as part 1 of **Company Law in Europe**. The local card is `_tools/publication-queue/company-law-in-europe-01.json`; the queue is Git-ignored, while the source and image remain in this Jekyll-excluded planning area.

**The angle already covered:** a skilled but cash-poor metalworker sees the new GmbH as a way to protect his pregnant wife and four children from his trade debts. He cannot assemble the required **20,000 marks of subscribed capital** or complete the formal formation and registration steps. He signs personally for iron, his buyer fails, and the unpaid debt costs the family their combined home and workshop. The post makes the *cost of being unable to incorporate* concrete; it does not claim that all 20,000 marks had to be paid in cash at once or that the law was written to exclude him.

**A distinct possible follow-up:** tell a standalone 1892 story from the **iron merchant's** point of view. He gives iron on credit and has workers or family to support; if his customer trades through a GmbH and its assets run out, the merchant generally cannot collect the company's debt from its members personally. This makes the 1892 capital, deed, and register rules a contest between two exposed households rather than a faceless bureaucratic obstacle. The [original Act, especially §§ 2, 5, 7, 11, and 13](https://de.wikisource.org/wiki/Gesetz,_betreffend_die_Gesellschaften_mit_beschr%C3%A4nkter_Haftung), establishes those mechanics. Before writing, check the legislative debates for the stated rationale and avoid portraying subscribed capital as cash permanently reserved for creditors. Give this merchant his own complete setting, stakes, and ending; readers must not need the founder's post to understand it.

A later German revisit could contrast the original 1892 threshold with the lower-capital UG introduced in 2008, after its reform history is verified. That episode would ask which barrier was lowered and which formalities remained.
